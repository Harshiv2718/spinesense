Note on sourcing: NICE (nice.org.uk) returned HTTP 403, and NCBI Bookshelf/PubMed returned bot checks for direct fetches. Trial and review abstracts were therefore read through the Europe PMC API, which serves the same PubMed abstracts. NICE content was read through secondary summaries, and each one is marked. "(older: YEAR)" marks sources from before 2022.

## 1. How big is the problem? (GBD burden, YLDs, 2050 projections, proportion "non-specific")

### Takeaway
LBP is the single leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) worldwide. In 2020, 619 million people had it, generating 69 million YLDs (8.1% of all YLDs). Cases are projected to reach 843 million by 2050, driven by population growth and ageing rather than by rising age-standardised rates. At least about 90% of primary-care cases are "non-specific"/chronic primary, with no identifiable pathoanatomical cause. These are exactly the people a guided exercise app targets.

### Cited Findings
- GBD 2021 (Lancet Rheumatology, 2023): 619 million (95% UI 554–694) prevalent cases globally in 2020, a 60.4% (57.1–64.2) increase in absolute cases since 1990 — [GBD 2021 LBP Collaborators, Lancet Rheumatol 2023 (PMC full text)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- Age-standardised prevalence in 2020 was 7,460 per 100,000 (6,690–8,370) — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- YLDs in 2020: 69.0 million (47.9–88.9), with an age-standardised YLD rate of 832 per 100,000 (578–1,070). LBP is "the leading cause of YLDs globally" and accounted for 8.1% (6.7–9.5) of all-cause YLDs — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- Projection: 843 million (759–933) prevalent cases by 2050, +36.4% (29.9–43.2) from 2020. Prevalence was projected by regressing prevalence rates on the Socio-demographic Index and multiplying by projected population — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/); [University of Malta repository abstract](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/111925)
- Age-standardised prevalence fell 10.4% and age-standardised YLD rate fell 10.5% over 1990–2020. The rise in absolute numbers is from population growth and ageing — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- The largest increases to 2050 are expected in Asia and Africa. Australia is projected to see a nearly 50% increase in cases (press release, secondary) — [University of Sydney news, May 2023](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2023/05/23/in-2050-over-800-million-people-estimated-to-have-back-pain-mode.html)
- Attributable risk: 38.8% (28.7–47.0) of LBP YLDs were attributable to modelled risk factors. Occupational ergonomic factors account for 22.0% (20.4–23.4), smoking 12.5% (3.1–21.5) and high BMI 11.5% (1.4–20.9) — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- Sex/age: age-standardised prevalence was 9,330 per 100,000 in females vs 5,520 in males. Prevalence is higher in females at all ages, most markedly above age 75 — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- GBD case definition: pain between the lower margin of the 12th ribs and the lower gluteal folds, with or without referred leg pain, lasting at least one day. The GBD paper does not report a non-specific proportion — [PMC10234592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234592/)
- WHO: LBP is "the leading cause of disability globally", affecting about 1 in 13 people (619 million) in 2020. Chronic primary LBP (i.e., non-specific) is "commonly estimated to represent at least 90% of cases" in primary care — [WHO news release, 7 Dec 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/07-12-2023-who-releases-guidelines-on-chronic-low-back-pain)
- IHME press release confirms that the GBD 2021 analysis identifies LBP as the leading cause of disability — [IHME/healthdata.org press release](https://www.healthdata.org/news-events/newsroom/news-releases/lancet-new-study-shows-low-back-pain-leading-cause-disability)

### Inferences
- The SpineSense target user (non-specific LBP, no red flags) is the overwhelming majority (≥90%) of people with LBP. A home-exercise tool fits the bulk of the clinical need.
- Ageing drives the growth, so older users will be a growing share. Sit-to-stand and hip hinge (functional, fall-relevant movements) are well matched. Webcam pose detection should be tested on older bodies and slower movement speeds.
- Prevalence is higher in women at all ages. Design and testing samples should reflect that.

### Gaps
- A 2025 Frontiers in Public Health GBD-2021 analysis of working-age adults (15–64) appeared in search results, but it was not fetched, so its figures are not reported here.
- I found no GBD 2023-round LBP-specific publication (GBD 2023 was released in 2025). The latest LBP-dedicated GBD paper located is GBD 2021 (data year 2020). GBD 2023 headline LBP numbers may exist in the main Lancet GBD 2023 capstone papers but were not retrieved.
- The "≥90% non-specific" figure is a WHO estimate ("commonly estimated"), not a pooled epidemiological measurement. I did not retrieve the Lancet 2018 Hartvigsen paper's exact wording.

## 2. What do the guidelines recommend in 2024–2026? (NICE NG59 incl. July 2026 update, WHO 2023, ACP 2017, Lancet 2018 series, JOSPT 2021)

### Takeaway
All major guidelines converge on the same pattern. First-line care is education/reassurance, self-management, staying active and structured exercise. Manual therapy is allowed only alongside exercise (NICE) or as an option (WHO/ACP). NSAIDs are the only consistently endorsed drug. Imaging, lumbar belts/supports, traction, TENS/ultrasound, opioids, antidepressants and anticonvulsants are not recommended. The main 2026 change: on 29 July 2026, NICE NG59 withdrew its psychological-therapy recommendations (1.2.13, 1.2.14), partly because their underlying evidence had been retracted. WHO 2023 still recommends CBT/operant therapy, so guidelines now diverge on stand-alone psychological therapy. Nothing in any guideline argues against exercise, which is the core of SpineSense.

### Cited Findings

**NICE NG59 (2016, last substantively updated 2020; amended July 2026)**
- 29 July 2026 update: recommendation 1.2.13 (cognitive behavioural psychological therapy as part of a package including exercise) and 1.2.14 (combined physical and psychological programme for persistent LBP/sciatica) were withdrawn. References to psychological approaches were removed from 1.1.3 (risk stratification) and 1.2.7 (manual therapy). NICE "did not withdraw the wider guideline" (secondary source; nice.org.uk could not be fetched to confirm) — [Physitrack summary, 2026](https://www.physitrack.com/insights/nice-low-back-pain-guideline-update-2026)
- Reason: the withdrawn recommendations "were partly based on evidence that was retracted since publication". An update was judged "unlikely to notably impact clinical practice". No replacement psychological intervention was named (secondary) — [Guideline Central, Aug 2026 NG59 spotlight](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/insights/aug-2026-nice-lowbackpainsciaticaover16-guideline-spotlight/)
- Current 1.1.3: consider risk stratification at first contact (e.g., STarT Back). Lower risk gets simpler support (reassurance, advice to keep active, self-management guidance). Higher risk gets more complex support (exercise programmes with or without manual therapy) — [Guideline Central 2026](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/insights/aug-2026-nice-lowbackpainsciaticaover16-guideline-spotlight/); [Medical Dialogues mirror of NG59](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)
- Current 1.2.7: consider spinal manipulation, mobilisation or soft-tissue techniques only as part of a package that includes exercise — [Guideline Central 2026](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/insights/aug-2026-nice-lowbackpainsciaticaover16-guideline-spotlight/)
- NG59 recommends (pre-2026 text, unaffected by the July 2026 change per Physitrack):
  - Tailored self-management advice and information at every step, encouraging normal activity.
  - Consider a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind–body or a combination) for a specific episode or flare-up, fitted to the person's needs and capabilities.
  - Promote return to work/normal activities.
  - Do not routinely offer imaging in non-specialist settings.

  Sources: [Medical Dialogues mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines); [Physitrack 2026](https://www.physitrack.com/insights/nice-low-back-pain-guideline-update-2026)
- NG59 "do not offer" (non-drug): belts or corsets (1.2.3), foot orthotics (1.2.4), rocker-sole shoes (1.2.5), traction (1.2.6), acupuncture (1.2.8), ultrasound (1.2.9), PENS (1.2.10), TENS (1.2.11), interferential therapy — [NICE NG59 recommendations page (via search index)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendation); [Medical Dialogues mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)
- NG59 drugs: consider oral NSAIDs at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Consider weak opioids only for acute LBP if NSAIDs are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective. Do NOT offer paracetamol alone, opioids routinely for acute LBP, opioids for chronic LBP, SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs, or anticonvulsants — [Medical Dialogues mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)
- The NICE page snapshot showed "last reviewed 11 December 2020". The 2020 update tightened prescribing advice, including that opioids should not be offered for chronic sciatica — [NICE NG59 landing page](https://www.nice.org.uk/ng59)

**WHO guideline (7 Dec 2023): non-surgical management of chronic primary LBP in adults, primary/community care**
- Scope: adults including older people, primary and community care. 242 pages, ISBN 978-92-4-008178-9, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO licence. Excludes secondary/tertiary-care interventions and workplace interventions — [WHO publication page](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240081789)
- Recommended: "education programs that support knowledge and self-care strategies", "exercise programs", spinal manipulative therapy and massage, psychological therapy including "cognitive behavioural therapy", and NSAIDs — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/07-12-2023-who-releases-guidelines-on-chronic-low-back-pain)
- Also recommended per secondary summaries: acupuncture/needling for all adults including older adults, and topical cayenne (Capsicum). "NSAIDs continue to be the only recommended pharmacotherapy" — [Acupuncture Today summary](https://acupuncturetoday.com/article/39209-new-guideline-on-chronic-lbp-global-impact)
- WHO lists 14 interventions not recommended "for most people in most contexts", including lumbar braces/belts/supports, traction and opioid analgesics — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/07-12-2023-who-releases-guidelines-on-chronic-low-back-pain)
- Further named "against" items (secondary): therapeutic ultrasound, TENS, pharmacological weight loss, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, skeletal muscle relaxants, glucocorticoids. The rationale is that "the possible harms outweigh the benefits". "Most of the evidence... was low certainty" and "many recommendations... are conditional" — [Healio, Dec 2023](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20231220/new-who-guidance-on-lower-back-pain-advises-against-tens-lumbar-support)
- Also against (secondary): TCAs and SNRIs, injectable local anaesthetics. No recommendation was made for paracetamol/acetaminophen, benzodiazepines or cannabis-related preparations — [Acupuncture Today](https://acupuncturetoday.com/article/39209-new-guideline-on-chronic-lbp-global-impact)

**ACP (Qaseem et al., Ann Intern Med 2017;166:514–530) (older: 2017; no newer ACP LBP guideline found)**
- Acute/subacute LBP: non-drug options first:
  - superficial heat (moderate-quality evidence)
  - massage (low)
  - acupuncture (low)
  - spinal manipulation (low)

  If a drug is used, NSAIDs or skeletal muscle relaxants — [AAFP summary of ACP 2017](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2017/0915/p407.html)
- Chronic LBP: non-drug options first:
  - exercise (minimal improvement in pain/function vs no exercise; moderate quality)
  - MBSR (moderate)
  - multidisciplinary rehab
  - Iyengar yoga
  - motor control exercise
  - acupuncture
  - tai chi
  - progressive relaxation
  - low-level laser
  - spinal manipulation
  - operant therapy/CBT/EMG biofeedback (all low quality)

  Drugs only if these fail: NSAIDs first-line, tramadol or duloxetine second-line. Opioids are considered only if other treatments fail and only after a benefit/risk discussion — [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2017/0915/p407.html)

**Lancet LBP series (2018) (older: 2018)**
- Guidelines broadly recommend a biopsychosocial framework with initial non-pharmacological treatment (education, self-management, staying active, exercise), psychological programmes for persistent symptoms, and "prudent use of medication, imaging, and surgery" — [Foster et al., Lancet 2018, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30489-6); [VU Amsterdam abstract page](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/prevention-and-treatment-of-low-back-pain-evidence-challenges-and/)
- Practice diverges from guidelines, with "inappropriately high use of imaging, rest, opioids, spinal injections, and surgery". Evidence comes almost exclusively from high-income countries — [VU abstract page](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/prevention-and-treatment-of-low-back-pain-evidence-challenges-and/)
- Secondary prevention: exercise alone or with education were the "only known effective interventions" (press coverage of the series) — [Healio, March 2018](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20180321/more-than-1-in-10-worldwide-suffer-lower-back-pain)

**JOSPT/AOPT physical therapy CPG, Revision 2021 (George et al., JOSPT 51(11):CPG1–CPG60)**
- Chronic LBP: exercise (including trunk strengthening/endurance and multimodal approaches) carries the strongest grade (A). Acute LBP: exercise including specific trunk muscle activation (grade C). Acute LBP with leg pain: trunk strengthening/endurance and specific trunk activation (grade B). Based on 177 source documents, search to 24 June 2020 — [PubMed 34719942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34719942/); [AOPT full-text PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/jospt.2021.0304.pdf)

### Inferences
- Safe app messaging (agrees with all guidelines): reassurance that LBP is common and usually not serious, stay active, keep working/doing normal activities, and do regular exercise the person enjoys.
- The app should NOT suggest or upsell: lumbar belts/braces/corsets, TENS, ultrasound, traction devices, foot orthotics/rocker shoes (NICE/WHO advise against), or any medicines. If it mentions medicines at all, it should only say "speak to a pharmacist/doctor"; opioids, antidepressants and anticonvulsants are advised against for LBP. Acupuncture is a known conflict: NICE advises against it, while WHO and ACP list it as an option.
- Psychological content: NICE withdrew stand-alone and combined psychological-therapy recommendations in July 2026 because of retracted evidence, while WHO 2023 still recommends CBT/operant therapy and CFT (Q3) has strong trial support. Light-touch, evidence-aligned reassurance and graded-exposure framing inside an exercise programme is consistent with every guideline. Marketing SpineSense as "psychological therapy" would not be.
- NICE suggests group exercise "for a specific episode or flare-up", so offering exercise during flares is guideline-consistent, scaled to tolerance.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch nice.org.uk directly (HTTP 403). The July 2026 changes rest on two consistent secondary sources (Physitrack, Guideline Central), and exact recommendation numbering for the drug "do not offer" items was not confirmed on the official page.
- I could not retrieve the full WHO list of 14 "not recommended" interventions, or which recommendations are conditional vs strong (NCBI Bookshelf blocked; the WHO PDF is 13 MB and was not parsed).
- ACP strength-of-recommendation grades and ACP statements on acetaminophen and systemic steroids were not confirmed from the primary Annals text. No ACP update after 2017 was found.
- I did not check for a 2024–2026 Lancet LBP series beyond 2018.

## 3. Which treatments have the best evidence? (exercise types, effect sizes, CFT, walking, yoga/Pilates, motor control, digital self-management)

### Takeaway
Exercise is "probably effective" for chronic LBP pain (moderate certainty): about 15 points on a 0–100 pain scale vs no treatment, but only about 7 points for function, below the clinical-importance threshold. No single exercise type is clearly superior. Pilates, McKenzie, functional restoration and motor control/stabilisation rank highest in network meta-analyses, but at low certainty, and authors recommend choosing exercise the person enjoys to aid adherence. The biggest recent effects come from cognitive functional therapy (RESTORE, Lancet 2023: −4.6 RMDQ points vs usual care, sustained to 3 years) and from walking for recurrence prevention (WalkBack, Lancet 2024: median 208 vs 112 days to recurrence, HR 0.72). App-delivered self-management alone (SELFBACK) gives only a small benefit (−0.79 RMDQ).

### Cited Findings

**Exercise in general (Cochrane)**
- Hayden et al. 2021 Cochrane review, 249 trials. Exercise vs no treatment/usual care/placebo at earliest follow-up: pain MD −15.2 (95% CI −18.3 to −12.2), moderate certainty, clinically important (threshold 15/100). Functional limitation MD −6.8 (−8.3 to −5.3), moderate certainty, below the 10-point threshold — [PubMed 34580864 (abstract read via Europe PMC)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580864/); [Cochrane Library record](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009790.pub2/information)
- Exercise vs other conservative treatments: pain MD −9.1 (−12.6 to −5.6), function MD −4.1 (−6.0 to −2.2). Neither is clinically important — [PubMed 34580864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580864/)
- Harms: adverse effects were reported in 37/112 exercise groups (33%) vs 12/42 comparison groups (29%), "mostly minor" (e.g., muscle soreness). Systematically measured harms had a median of 0.14 per participant (exercise) vs 0.12 (comparison) — [PubMed 34580864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580864/)

**Which type of exercise (network meta-analyses)**
- Hayden et al. 2021 NMA (J Physiother), 217 RCTs, 20,969 participants. Pilates, McKenzie therapy and functional restoration (pain), plus flexibility exercise (function), vs minimal treatment: MD −15 to −19 for pain and −10 to −12 for functional limitation (0–100). "Pilates, McKenzie therapy and functional restoration were more effective than other types of exercise treatment". The authors encourage doing exercise people enjoy, to promote adherence — [Europe PMC abstract query](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(TITLE:%22Some%20types%20of%20exercise%20are%20more%20effective%20than%20others%22)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Yoga%20treatment%20for%20chronic%20non-specific%20low%20back%20pain%22%20AND%20PUB_YEAR:2022)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Motor%20control%20exercise%20for%20chronic%20non-specific%20low-back%20pain%22%20AND%20PUB_YEAR:2016)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Which%20specific%20modes%20of%20exercise%20training%22)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22smartphone%20app-delivered%20self-management%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22SELFBACK%22)&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=10); [PEDro summary](https://pedro.org.au/?p=21068)
- Owen et al. 2020 NMA (BJSM), 89 studies, 5,578 patients. SMDs vs true control:
  - Pilates for pain: −1.86 (−2.54 to −1.19), SUCRA 100%
  - Resistance for physical function: −1.14 (−1.71 to −0.56)
  - Stabilisation/motor control for function: −1.13 (−1.53 to −0.74)
  - Resistance for mental health: −1.26 (−2.10 to −0.41)
  - Aerobic for mental health: −1.18 (−2.20 to −0.15)

  Evidence was "low quality" per GRADE. An Expression of Concern was later rescinded — [PubMed 31666220](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31666220/); [BJSM abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/21/1279.abstract)
- Li et al. 2023 NMA (Frontiers in Public Health), 75 RCTs, 5,254 participants, 20 exercise types. Tai chi, yoga, Pilates and sling exercise beat conventional rehab for pain. Motor control and core/stabilisation also significantly improved outcomes vs conventional rehab and no intervention — [Frontiers Public Health 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1155225/full)

**Motor control/stabilisation (relevant to bird dog, glute bridge; McGill-style)**
- Saragiotto et al. 2016 Cochrane (older: 2016), 29 trials, n=2,431. Motor control exercise vs minimal intervention, long-term pain MD −12.97 (−18.51 to −7.42), low-to-moderate quality. Moderate-to-high quality evidence of no clinically important difference vs manual therapy, and low-to-high quality evidence of no important difference vs other exercise. "MCE is not superior to other forms of exercise", so choice should depend on preference, training, cost and safety — [Europe PMC abstract query](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(TITLE:%22Some%20types%20of%20exercise%20are%20more%20effective%20than%20others%22)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Yoga%20treatment%20for%20chronic%20non-specific%20low%20back%20pain%22%20AND%20PUB_YEAR:2022)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Motor%20control%20exercise%20for%20chronic%20non-specific%20low-back%20pain%22%20AND%20PUB_YEAR:2016)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22Which%20specific%20modes%20of%20exercise%20training%22)%20OR%20(TITLE:%22smartphone%20app-delivered%20self-management%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22SELFBACK%22)&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=10)

**Yoga**
- Cochrane 2022, 21 RCTs, 2,223 participants. Yoga vs no exercise at 3 months: pain MD −4.53 (−6.61 to −2.46)/100 (moderate certainty, "clinically unimportant"); RMDQ MD −1.69 (−2.73 to −0.65) (low certainty); clinical improvement RR 2.33 (1.46–3.71). Adverse events were higher with yoga than no exercise: RR 4.76 (2.08–10.89), 43 vs 9 per 1,000. Yoga vs other back exercise: little or no difference in function (MD −0.38, −1.33 to 0.62) — [Cochrane yoga review 2022 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010671.pub3)

**Cognitive functional therapy (CFT)**
- RESTORE (Kent et al., Lancet 2023), n=492 (164 CFT, 163 CFT+biofeedback, 165 usual care). RMDQ at 13 weeks: CFT vs usual care MD −4.6 (−5.9 to −3.4); CFT+biofeedback MD −4.6 (−5.8 to −3.3). Effects were similar at 52 weeks. Both arms gained more QALYs and had lower societal costs than usual care. Biofeedback sensors added nothing — [PubMed 37146623 (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37146623/)
- 3-year follow-up (Lancet Rheumatology 2025): results "largely maintained" (secondary) — [News-Medical, Aug 2025](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20250805/Australian-study-finds-lasting-relief-for-back-pain-through-cognitive-functional-therapy.aspx)

**Walking (secondary prevention)**
- WalkBack (Pocovi et al., Lancet 2024), n=701 adults recently recovered from non-specific LBP. The intervention was an individualised, progressive walking programme plus education, with 6 physio sessions over 6 months, vs no treatment. Time to first activity-limiting recurrence: HR 0.72 (0.60–0.85), p=0.0002; median 208 days (149–295) vs 112 (89–140). AU$7,802 per QALY, 94% probability of cost-effectiveness. Any adverse event: 52% vs 54% (NS), but more lower-extremity AEs (100 vs 54) — [PubMed 38908392 (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38908392/)
- Activity-limiting recurrences fell by 28%, and care-seeking recurrences by 43%. The participants were mostly female, aged 43–66 and well educated (secondary) — [HealthEd summary](https://healthed.com.au/clinical_articles/walking-can-prevent-low-back-pain-a-new-study-shows); [Macquarie WalkBack page](https://mq.edu.au/research/research-centres-groups-and-facilities/groups/spinal-pain-research-group/our-projects/walkback-trial)

**App-delivered self-management (closest analogue to SpineSense)**
- SELFBACK RCT (Sandal et al., JAMA Intern Med 2021, Denmark/Norway), n=461. App-tailored self-management (exercise, education, activity) on top of usual care. RMDQ at 3 months: adjusted MD 0.79 (95% CI 0.06–1.51) favouring the app, "small and of uncertain clinical significance". Responders (≥4-point RMDQ improvement): 52% vs 39%, OR 1.76 (1.15–2.70). 87% completed 3-month follow-up — [SELFBACK, JAMA Intern Med 2021 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.4097)
- 2024 review of LBP apps (16 studies): of 6 RCTs, 4 showed significant pain reductions vs control. "Few apps meet satisfying quality, content, and functionality criteria" — [DOAJ record, Life (Jun 2024)](https://doaj.org/article/7edbc9333d694e098d9ad8e9d7fa4cdd)
- UK app-store analysis (2024): 69 LBP apps, mean MARS quality score 2.4/5 — [DOAJ record, JMIR mHealth uHealth (Feb 2024)](https://doaj.org/article/24a6fba7171747d5b5b0a0e23b4201f5)

### Inferences
- The SpineSense exercise set maps onto evidence-supported categories:
  - bird dog and glute bridge: motor control/stabilisation/strengthening
  - hip hinge and sit-to-stand: functional restoration/resistance
  - arm raise and side bend: mobility/flexibility
  - knee-to-chest: flexion-biased mobility

  No specific variant is proven superior, so variety, user choice and enjoyment are evidence-backed design levers.
- Gaps in the current set relative to the evidence:
  - no walking/aerobic component (WalkBack is the strongest recurrence-prevention trial)
  - no extension-direction movement (McKenzie ranked highly; see Q7 on directional preference)
  - no progressive loading/resistance progression

  Consider adding a walking goal/tracker and progressive difficulty levels.
- Realistic expectation setting: the effect of exercise alone on pain is moderate and on function small. An app alone (SELFBACK) gives small benefits. The larger effects (CFT) combine movement with changing beliefs about pain and graded exposure to feared movements, which an app can partly emulate through education and confidence-building feedback.
- Biofeedback added nothing over CFT in RESTORE. Real-time sensor/visual feedback is therefore unlikely to be an efficacy driver by itself. Its value is more plausibly in adherence, engagement and safe dosing (an inference, not tested).

### Gaps
- Point estimates with CIs for each exercise type in the Hayden 2021 NMA were not available in the abstract (only ranges).
- I found no RCT evidence specifically for McGill "Big 3" stabilisation as a distinct protocol; it falls under motor control/stabilisation evidence.
- I found no trial of webcam/computer-vision-guided home exercise for LBP with clinical outcomes. Graded activity (operant) as a stand-alone exercise approach was not separately quantified.

## 4. What are the biggest barriers to recovery? (fear-avoidance/kinesiophobia, catastrophising, home-exercise adherence, flare-ups, recurrence)

### Takeaway
The main clinical obstacles are psychological (fear-avoidance beliefs, kinesiophobia, catastrophising, which predict persistence and poorer return to work), behavioural (poor home-exercise adherence: non-adherence reaches about 50–70%, and LBP-specific adherence ranges 8–91%), and natural history (high recurrence: 69% within a year in one inception cohort). Strategies tested to boost long-term adherence have mostly failed. The strongest predictors of adherence are self-efficacy, intention, self-motivation, prior exercise habits and social support.

### Cited Findings

**Adherence numbers**
- Systematic review of LBP home-exercise/activity-advice adherence (van Koppen et al., Eur J Physiother 2022), 28 studies: "Adherence rates varied from 8% to 91%". "The proportion of fully adhering patients is unclear". The subjective measurement tools used compromised the findings — [Maastricht University record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/adherence-to-home-based-exercises-andor-activity-advice-in-low-ba)
- Essery et al. 2016 systematic review (Disabil Rehabil 39(6):519–534), 30 quantitative articles: "non-adherence can reach 70%" for self-managed home-based physical therapy. Relatively strong evidence that adherence is predicted by intention to exercise, self-motivation, self-efficacy, prior adherence to exercise-related behaviours and social support — [Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A3bc02ac3-10c1-432f-a472-34347cb57203)
- A scoping review reports that non-adherence to home exercise programmes ranges from 50% to 70% (citing primary studies) — [Physiotherapy Quarterly scoping review](https://physioquart.awf.wroc.pl/Exploring-factors-influencing-adherence-to-home-exercise-program-among-patients-with,202671,0,2.html)
- Another review reports home-programme adherence between 35% and 84%, with LBP adherence described as inadequate — [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2024 (s12891-024-07705-6)](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12891-024-07705-6.pdf)
- McLean et al. 2010 systematic review (older: 2010): strong evidence that adherence-enhancing strategies are not effective at improving long-term home-exercise adherence — [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/2353/)
- A small chronic LBP study (n=40) defining adherence as exercising ≥3 days/week found 77.5% adherent. The main non-adherence reason was lack of time (75% of non-adherers) — [SDU repository](https://acikerisim.sdu.edu.tr/handle/123456789/24156)
- In a Brazilian primary-care sample, higher pain intensity and psychosocial factors were associated with non-adherence — [Brazilian Journal of Pain (SciELO)](https://www.scielo.br/j/brjp/a/qKwsfNMDD4J5Qs5LYPn6DNy/abstract/?lang=en)
- In LBP apps, decision-support features (personalised feedback, push notifications) were reported to benefit engagement, but the link between retention and clinical outcomes was unclear (Lewkowicz et al., JMIR Rehabil Assist Technol 2021) — [PMC8663573](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8663573/)
- Hayden 2021 NMA authors recommend that people do exercise they enjoy, to promote adherence — [PEDro summary](https://pedro.org.au/?p=21068)

**Fear-avoidance, kinesiophobia, catastrophising**
- Wertli et al. 2014 systematic review (Spine J) (older: 2014): in back pain of up to 6 months, high fear-avoidance beliefs were associated with more pain/disability (4 RCTs) and less return to work (3 RCTs), high-quality GRADE evidence — [Spine J 2014, DOI 10.1016/j.spinee.2013.09.036](https://read.qxmd.com/doi/10.1016/j.spinee.2013.09.036)
- Picavet et al. 2002 population cohort (older: 2002): high pain catastrophising and high kinesiophobia predicted LBP and chronic LBP at follow-up, independent of baseline pain duration, severity and disability — [PubMed 12446259](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12446259/)
- Fear-avoidance beliefs have also been studied as a moderator of treatment efficacy (Wertli et al. 2014) — [PubMed 24614254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24614254/)
- The evidence on fear as an early prognostic factor is contested. An older review found no link between baseline fear and 3- or 12-month outcomes in early-stage LBP, but some evidence once pain persists (search summary of Pincus et al. 2006, older: 2006). The source URL was not separately verified, so treat this as low-confidence — see Gaps
- TSK cut-offs: >37 was originally used to identify high kinesiophobia (Vlaeyen). Later work suggests >~40 — [Physiotutors TSK page](https://www.physiotutors.com/questionnaires/tampa-scale-kinesiophobia/)
- A systematic review found conservative interventions potentially effective at reducing kinesiophobia and fear-avoidance beliefs in chronic LBP — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1213199/pdf)

**Recurrence and flares**
- da Silva et al. 2019 inception cohort (J Physiother) (older: 2019), n=250 recently recovered from LBP. 69% had a recurrence within 1 year, 40% had an activity-limiting recurrence and 41% sought care. Risk factors: frequent awkward postures, longer sitting time and >2 previous episodes — [FAPESP record](https://bv.fapesp.br/en/publicacao/221868); [Noigroup summary](https://www.noigroup.com/estimates-of-risk-of-recurrence-of-low-back-pain-are-much-higher-than-previously-reported/)
- An earlier systematic review found only 1 inception cohort (1-year recurrence 33%) and concluded robust recurrence estimates were lacking (older: ~2017) — [Macquarie record: Risk of recurrence of LBP, systematic review](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/risk-of-recurrence-of-low-back-pain-a-systematic-review/)
- Consensus flare definition (Costa et al. 2019, Delphi with 130 consumers, 19 experts and 50 Delphi panellists) (older: 2019): a flare is "a worsening of your condition that lasts from hours to weeks that is difficult to tolerate and generally impacts your usual activities and/or emotions" — [Macquarie record: A definition of flare in LBP](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/a-definition-of-flare-in-low-back-pain-a-multiphase-process-invol/); [PubMed 30904517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30904517/)
- NICE suggests a group exercise programme for "a specific episode or flare-up" — [Medical Dialogues NG59 mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)
- WalkBack: walking plus education nearly doubled the median time to an activity-limiting recurrence (208 vs 112 days; HR 0.72) (see Q3) — [PubMed 38908392](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38908392/)

### Inferences
- Design for non-adherence as the default. Plan on roughly half or more of users not sustaining a home programme. Since generic adherence strategies fail long-term (McLean 2010), target the factors that do predict adherence (Essery): self-efficacy (early wins, visible progress, achievable doses), intention/goal setting (PSFS-style personal goals), habit/prior behaviour (fixed daily slot, short sessions, "lack of time" is the top reason), and social support (optional sharing).
- Fear-avoidance is the key psychological target. Rep-by-rep praise for successful movement, graded exposure (e.g., small-range hip hinge progressing to full range) and "hurt ≠ harm" education are compatible with the CFT model. Avoid alarming language ("your spine is unstable", "bad posture damages discs") that could reinforce kinesiophobia.
- An optional TSK screen, or a simple one-item fear question, could flag high-fear users for gentler progression and more education.
- Recurrence is the norm (up to 69%/year), so the app should frame itself as a long-term maintenance tool with a flare-up plan: reduce load, keep moving, use gentle movements and walking, and resume progression when the flare settles.

### Gaps
- No pooled, standardised adherence percentage exists. Reviews emphasise heterogeneous and subjective measurement.
- I did not retrieve LBP-specific prevalence of high kinesiophobia (e.g., % of chronic LBP patients with TSK >37), nor a dedicated systematic review of catastrophising as a prognostic factor.
- I found no data on flare frequency per year, or on trial evidence for specific flare-management protocols.
- The Pincus 2006 review claim came from a search summary without a verified URL; it should be checked before citing.

## 5. Is pain during exercise acceptable? (pain-monitoring / traffic-light rules)

### Takeaway
Yes, within limits. A meta-analysis found exercise that allows or encourages pain is at least as good as pain-free exercise, with a small short-term pain advantage (SMD −0.27). The most widely used operational rule is the pain-monitoring model (from Achilles tendinopathy rehab). Pain during or after exercise up to about 5/10 is allowed if it settles by the next morning/within 24 h and does not build week to week. A common traffic-light split is 0–2 "safe", 2–5 "acceptable" and >5 "high risk". LBP-specific validation of exact thresholds is lacking.

### Cited Findings
- Smith et al. 2017 systematic review/meta-analysis (BJSM 51(23):1679–1687) (older: 2017), 9 papers (7 RCTs), 385 participants with chronic musculoskeletal pain. Painful vs pain-free exercise, short-term pain SMD −0.27 (−0.54 to −0.05) favouring painful exercise (moderate-quality evidence). No clear difference at medium/long term, and no difference for function/disability. "Pain during therapeutic exercise need not be a barrier to successful outcomes" — [BJSM 2017](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/23/1679); [UEA repository](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/63158/)
- Pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel, Achilles tendinopathy) (older: 2001/2007): pain allowed to reach 5/10 on a VAS during/after exercise but should subside by the following morning. Pain and stiffness should not increase week to week (secondary summary) — [PhysicalTherapyFirst summary](https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/continued-sports-activity-using-a-pain-monitoring-model-during-rehabilitation-in-patients-with-achilles-tendinopathy/)
- Back-off criteria in the model: reduce load if pain doesn't decrease after stopping exercise, is still present the next morning, or if pain/stiffness increase the day after — [Physio Field Guide summary](https://www.patreon.com/physiofieldguide/posts/midportion-75112715)
- Traffic-light framing: 0–2 "safe", 2–5 "acceptable", >5 "high risk". Thresholds have been applied differently across studies — [First Principles of Movement blog](https://firstprinciplesofmovement.com/?p=3733)
- LBP-specific application: a primary-care best-practice LBP model protocol describes keeping activity within tolerable pain. Pain may increase within acceptable limits during/after training but should return to baseline within 24 h, and the patient should consult a physiotherapist if unsure — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03147300 protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/00/NCT03147300/Prot_SAP_000.pdf)
- Experimental trunk-extension DOMS model in healthy volunteers (Bishop et al. 2011) (older: 2011): peak soreness at 24 h in 57.5% and at 48 h in 32.5% of participants. Not everyone settles within 24 h after novel trunk loading — [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/1471-2474-12-35)
- Supporting safety data: in the Cochrane exercise review, adverse effects were mostly minor (e.g., muscle soreness) and occurred at similar rates to comparison groups (33% vs 29% of groups reporting AEs) — [PubMed 34580864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580864/)

### Inferences
Proposed SpineSense pain rule, derived from the sources above but not validated for LBP:
- Before the session: ask current pain 0–10 (NPRS). If ≥8/10, or if red-flag answers are positive, do not start. Show a safety message instead.
- During the session:
  - Green (0–2, or no rise above baseline): continue and progress.
  - Amber (3–5, or a moderate rise): continue but reduce range, reps or tempo, and offer an easier variant.
  - Red (>5/10, sharp/shooting pain, or new/spreading leg symptoms): stop that exercise and switch to gentle movement or rest.
- After the session / next day: ask "Is your pain back to your usual level?" If not back to baseline by the next morning (within ~24 h), reduce the next session's dose. If symptoms build across a week, hold progression.
- Allow 24–48 h for unaccustomed trunk-exercise soreness (Bishop). Early sessions after introducing a new exercise should progress conservatively.
- Facial-expression "discomfort detection" should be treated as a prompt to ask the user for a rating, not as a measurement of pain. The self-reported rating remains the decision variable, consistent with how all the models above are specified.
- Educational framing ("some discomfort is normal and not harmful; it should settle by tomorrow") aligns with the meta-analytic evidence and with reducing fear-avoidance.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT validating the pain-monitoring model or a specific numeric ceiling (e.g., ≤5/10) specifically in LBP. The thresholds are extrapolated from tendinopathy and general musculoskeletal pain.
- The original Silbernagel 2001/2007 papers were not retrieved directly; the rules come from secondary summaries.
- I found no evidence on the validity of webcam facial-expression pain detection in LBP exercise.

## 6. Which outcome measures should an app track, and which are free? (ODI, RMDQ, NPRS, PSFS, TSK, STarT Back)

### Takeaway
The best free core set is RMDQ (disability, public domain, adaptable for online use), NPRS (pain) and PSFS (personal goals; licensing not verified here). Use agreed minimal important change values to show "meaningful improvement": NPRS 2 points, RMDQ 5 points (some sources say 2–3), ODI 10 points, or 30% from baseline on any of them. ODI is free only for individual clinical/unfunded academic use. Commercial and IT/digital users must request a licence from Mapi Research Trust (a fee may apply). The STarT Back Tool is free for non-commercial use, but Keele asks commercial product developers to contact them. TSK's licensing status is unclear.

### Cited Findings
- **RMDQ (Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire)**, 24 items, score 0–24 = number of items checked. The original and all translations are in the public domain. No permission is needed for use or reproduction and there is no charge. The questionnaire "may be adapted for use on-line or by telephone" — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab RehabMeasures entry](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/roland-morris-disability-questionnaire); [original RMDQ document](https://www.sralab.org/sites/default/files/2017-06/English%20%28original%29.doc)
- **ODI (Oswestry Disability Index) v2.1a**, owned by Jeremy Fairbank and distributed by Mapi Research Trust. Clinical and academic users can obtain a licence at no charge, and "commercial investigators will be asked to pay an affordable license fee". v2.1a is required for new studies — [Mapi Research Trust news](https://www.mapi-trust.org/news-events/news/the-oswestry-disability-i); [PhenX Toolkit ODI protocol](https://phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/220601)
- ODI is free for students, physicians, clinical practices and unfunded academic users. Funded academic users, healthcare organisations, commercial users and IT companies must request a licence agreement — [Scientific Spine ODI page](https://www.scientificspine.com/spine-scores/oswestry-disability-index.html)
- **STarT Back Screening Tool (Keele)**: free for non-commercial use without permission. To incorporate it into commercial product materials, contact Keele's Impact Accelerator Unit. Keele does not recommend modified versions for conditions other than LBP. The newer STarT MSK Tool requires a licence (free or paid) for all users — [Keele STarT Back privacy/legal page](https://startback.hfac.keele.ac.uk/privacy-policy); [Keele STarT Back research page](https://startback.hfac.keele.ac.uk/research); [Keele STarT MSK legal](https://www.keele.ac.uk/startmsk/legal/)
- NICE NG59 recommends considering risk stratification "for example, the STarT Back risk assessment tool" at first contact for each new episode — [Medical Dialogues NG59 mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)
- **TSK (Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia)**: the developer retains intellectual property. Cut-offs are >37 (Vlaeyen) or >~40 (Lundberg) for high kinesiophobia — [Physiotutors TSK page](https://www.physiotutors.com/questionnaires/tampa-scale-kinesiophobia/)
- **Minimal important change (Ostelo et al., Spine 2008 consensus)** (older: 2008): VAS 15/100, NRS 2 points, RMDQ 5 points, ODI 10 points, or a 30% improvement from baseline as a useful threshold across measures — [McMaster Experts record (Ostelo 2008)](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1540945)
- Alternative RMDQ thresholds: a 2–3 point change (Roland/Bombardier) — [IWH, MCID for LBP outcome measures (Bombardier 2001)](https://iwh.on.ca/node/1709). A 2006 derivation study used a 30% RMDQ reduction plus a "better" global rating — [PubMed 16360560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16360560/)
- A 2022 study (Bråten et al.) in chronic LBP with Modic changes found MIC thresholds "on the lower spectrum of previous estimates" — [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/minimal-important-change-was-on-the-lower-spectrum-of-previous-es/)
- Trial precedent: RESTORE used RMDQ as its primary outcome — [PubMed 37146623](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37146623/). SELFBACK used RMDQ (eligibility ≥6) with a ≥4-point responder threshold — [JAMA Intern Med 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.4097). WalkBack used days to activity-limiting recurrence — [PubMed 38908392](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38908392/)

### Inferences
Recommended SpineSense measurement stack:
- Every session: NPRS 0–10 before and after, plus a next-day "back to baseline?" check. This feeds the pain rules in Q5.
- Weekly: PSFS-style personal activity goals (e.g., "put on socks", "sit 30 min"), rated 0–10.
- Every 2–4 weeks: RMDQ (public domain, permitted online).
- Onboarding (optional): STarT Back for risk stratification. It is free for non-commercial use; a free non-commercial app should still contact Keele before embedding it.
- Long term: track flare/recurrence events (date, severity, activity limitation) to mirror the WalkBack outcome.

Further implications:
- Show progress against MIC thresholds (e.g., "Your RMDQ improved 5 points / 30%: that's a clinically meaningful change") to build self-efficacy, which predicts adherence (Q4).
- Avoid ODI unless a Mapi licence is obtained. A web app is likely an "IT company/commercial" use under ODI terms, even if free.
- Rep counts and form scores from pose tracking are process measures, not validated clinical outcomes. Present them as engagement metrics, alongside validated PROMs.

### Gaps
- PSFS and NPRS licensing were not verified in this session. Both are widely described as free, but no authoritative licence statement was retrieved.
- STarT Back scoring rules (cut-offs for low/medium/high risk) were not retrieved in this session. They should be taken from Keele's official scoring guide.
- TSK reuse permissions and the developer's terms remain unclear.
- I found no validation of these PROMs when self-administered inside a webcam exercise app (vs paper or clinical web forms).

## 7. Red flags, when to refer, and directional preference/centralisation (McKenzie) for exercise selection

### Takeaway
Most red flags have poor diagnostic accuracy individually, so the international (IFOMPT 2020) framework treats them as part of clinical reasoning rather than a checklist. Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is the critical emergency. Any back or leg pain with recent-onset (<2 weeks) bladder/bowel/saddle/genital sensory change, sexual dysfunction or severe or progressive leg weakness needs emergency referral, and a negative exam does not exclude it. For exercise selection, directional preference (about 70% prevalence in one review, 26% in another) and centralisation (about 40–44%) are positive prognostic signs. McKenzie ranks among the more effective exercise types in an NMA, but there is no evidence these signs modify treatment effect, and reliability is inconsistent.

### Cited Findings

**Red flags and referral**
- IFOMPT international framework for red flags for potential serious spinal pathologies (Finucane et al., JOSPT 2020) (older: 2020): a clinical-reasoning pathway. There is "an absence of high-quality evidence for the diagnostic accuracy of most red flags" — [Macquarie record of Finucane et al. 2020](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/international-framework-for-red-flags-for-potential-serious-spina/)
- UK National Suspected CES Pathway (GIRFT 2023), emergency referral triggers. Back and/or leg pain plus recent onset (<2 weeks) of any of:
  - "New difficulty initiating, or impaired sensation of, urinary flow"
  - "New altered sensation around the perianal, perineal or genital areas"
  - "Severe or progressive lower limb neurological deficits"
  - "New loss of sensation of rectal fullness"
  - "New sexual dysfunction"

  [CSP Physiotherapy Journal, March 2024 summary of the pathway](https://www.csp.org.uk/journal/article/physiotherapy-journal-march-2024/national-suspected-cauda-equina-syndrome-pathway)
- Warning signs needing urgent (not emergency) MSK referral within 2 weeks: sudden-onset bilateral leg pain, or unilateral leg pain spreading to both legs or switching sides, or static CES symptoms lasting >2 weeks. "Negative examination findings do not exclude CES if positive subjective symptoms are present". "CES does not have a set clinical pattern". Safety-netting with CES warning cards/videos is recommended — [CSP 2024](https://www.csp.org.uk/journal/article/physiotherapy-journal-march-2024/national-suspected-cauda-equina-syndrome-pathway); [BACPAR summary](https://bacpar.csp.org.uk/node/1344913)
- After telephone assessment, it "is not necessary to examine the patient face-to-face prior to making an emergency referral", which supports remote (including app-based) triage-to-emergency pathways — [CSP 2024](https://www.csp.org.uk/journal/article/physiotherapy-journal-march-2024/national-suspected-cauda-equina-syndrome-pathway)
- NICE NG59: imaging is not routinely offered in non-specialist settings; specialist imaging only if "the result is likely to change management" — [Medical Dialogues NG59 mirror](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/amp/low-back-pain-and-sciatica-assessment-and-management-guidelines)

**Directional preference (DP) and centralisation**
- May & Aina 2012 systematic review (older: 2012), 62 studies:
  - Centralisation prevalence 44.4% (range 11–89%) in 4,745 patients, more common in acute (74%) than subacute/chronic (42%).
  - DP prevalence 70% (60–78%) in 2,368 patients (5 studies).
  - 21/23 studies supported centralisation's prognostic validity; DP prognostic data came from only 1 study.
  - Reliability kappa 0.15–0.9.

  [PubMed 22695365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22695365/)
- Updated review: centralisation 40%, DP 26%. Both are "key positive prognostic factors" in LBP, but there is "no evidence that these might be important treatment effect modifiers" — [Chiro.org reproduction of the updated systematic review](https://chiro.org/Graphics_Box_LOW_BACK/Centralization_and_Directional_Preference.shtml)
- Long et al. 2004 RCT (Spine) (older: 2004): 229 participants with DP randomised to matched (McKenzie end-range direction-specific), opposite or evidence-based care exercises for 2 weeks. PEDro score 8/10, unblinded — [PEDro record 10836](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/10836); [Cochrane exercise review trial table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8477273/table/CD009790-tblf-0144)
- A case series reported improvement in 84% with DP-matched care vs 22% with unmatched care. It had no control group — [PMC2792793](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2792793)
- Hennemann et al. 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis: McKenzie for chronic LBP with DP, 5 RCTs, n=743 (abstract findings not retrieved) — [BVS record mdl-39383118](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/pt/mdl-39383118)
- The Hayden 2021 NMA ranks McKenzie among the most effective exercise types (pain MD −15 to −19/100 vs minimal treatment for the top group) — [PEDro summary](https://pedro.org.au/?p=21068)

### Inferences
Red-flag screen at onboarding, and repeated periodically (e.g., weekly or at any flare), as a yes/no checklist:
- Any CES symptom (bladder/bowel change, saddle/genital numbness, new sexual dysfunction, progressive leg weakness): show "Seek emergency care now (A&E/911/112)". Do not offer exercise. Show a CES warning card.
- Bilateral or alternating leg pain/sciatica: advise urgent clinician review within 2 weeks before exercising.
- Other serious-pathology flags were not specifically retrieved here (see Gaps), e.g., significant trauma, cancer history with new back pain, fever/unwell, unexplained weight loss, or pain unrelieved by rest/at night. Prompt the user to see a doctor.

The disclaimer should state that the app does not diagnose and that negative answers do not exclude serious pathology.

In-session neuro/peripheralisation stop rule: if the user reports new leg pain, numbness or tingling, or pain moving further down the leg during an exercise, stop that exercise. Peripheralisation is the opposite of centralisation, which is the favourable prognostic sign. This is an inference from the centralisation literature, not a sourced rule.

Directional-preference-informed selection (optional, low-certainty): a short repeated-movement test (e.g., 10 standing back-bends vs 10 knee-to-chest/forward flexions) could identify users whose symptoms centralise or ease with one direction. SpineSense could then bias exercise choice toward that direction, e.g., knee-to-chest for flexion, plus an added prone press-up/standing extension for extension. Evidence supports DP/centralisation as prognostic, but not as a treatment-effect modifier, and reliability is variable. Treat this as personalisation for comfort and confidence, not as a diagnostic claim.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the full IFOMPT red-flag tables (e.g., malignancy, fracture, infection criteria with conditional probabilities), or the full GIRFT pathway document (only the CSP summary).
- Diagnostic accuracy of CES red flags vs MRI was not obtained (the WCPT abstract was found but not read).
- I did not retrieve primary abstract numbers for Lam 2018 (MDT meta-analysis) or Hennemann 2024. Claims that MDT benefits chronic but not acute LBP came only from a consumer site and are unverified.
- The prevalence of DP (26% vs 70%) differs widely between reviews, reflecting definitions and populations.
